Dog Training 101: How to Teach Any Dog the Basics

The four principles behind every skill, and how to use them to teach "sit" in a single session.

2 min read · Updated November 2026

Every skill, from sit to a perfect recall, is taught the same way. Once you understand these principles, you can teach your dog almost anything.

The four principles

  1. Reward what you want, the moment it happens. Dogs repeat what pays off. Timing matters: reward within a second or two, or use a marker word like "yes!" to mark the exact moment. See clicker training.
  2. Make it easy, then harder. Start somewhere quiet, close and simple. Add the "three Ds" one at a time: distance, duration and distraction.
  3. Keep it short. Three to five minutes, a few times a day. End while your dog is still keen.
  4. Manage mistakes. If your dog can't get it wrong, they can't practice the wrong thing. Use leashes, gates and setups that help them succeed.

Teaching "sit": the lure method

  1. Hold a treat at your dog's nose.
  2. Slowly move it up and back over their head. As their head goes up, their bottom goes down.
  3. The moment they sit, say "yes!" and give the treat.
  4. Repeat 5–10 times.
  5. Fade the lure: do the same hand movement with no treat in it, and reward from your other hand.
  6. Add the word "sit" just before the hand signal once your dog is sitting reliably.
  7. Practice everywhere: kitchen, garden, on walks, before meals and before opening doors.

What to teach first

  • Name and attention: see getting your dog's attention
  • Sit, then down
  • Come when called
  • Leave it and drop it
  • Stay
  • Loose-leash walking

Track progress

Use our free printable training log. When your dog gets a skill right 8 times out of 10, make it a little harder.

Common mistakes

  • Repeating the cue ("sit, sit, SIT"). Say it once, then help your dog succeed.
  • Moving on too fast to a busy environment.
  • Training only when your dog is hungry and wound up, or exhausted.
  • Rewarding too late.

This guide is general information, not veterinary advice. If your dog is sick, hurt or acting unusually, call your veterinarian.
