Resource guarding is when a dog tries to keep something valuable from others: food, bones, toys, stolen items, a bed or even a person. It's a natural behavior, but it can lead to bites, especially with children.

## Signs

- Eating faster or freezing when someone approaches
- Hard stare, whale eye (showing the whites of the eyes), lip lifting
- Growling, snapping or biting
- Running off with items or hovering over them

## Safety first

- **Don't take things away** or reach toward a guarding dog.
- **Don't punish growling.** It's a warning. Punishing it can teach your dog to skip the warning and bite.
- **Manage:** feed your dog alone, in a crate or behind a gate. Pick up high-value chews when kids or other dogs are around.
- **Children should never approach a dog** that's eating or chewing.

## Changing how your dog feels

The goal is to teach your dog that people approaching their stuff means **good things are coming**, not that they're about to lose something.

1. **Start at a distance** where your dog notices you but stays relaxed.
2. **Walk past and toss a high-value treat** (better than what they have) near their bowl, then keep walking.
3. **Repeat over many sessions,** gradually getting closer as your dog stays relaxed, or even looks happy to see you.
4. **Practice trades** with low-value items: offer a great treat, praise when they drop the item, then give it back. See [drop it](/teach-your-dog-to-leave-it/).

## Get professional help if

- Your dog has bitten, or the guarding is intense
- There are children in the home
- Your dog guards against other dogs and fights have happened
- You don't feel safe doing the exercises

A qualified, reward-based trainer or veterinary behaviorist can create a safe plan. Ask your vet to check for pain, which can make guarding worse.
