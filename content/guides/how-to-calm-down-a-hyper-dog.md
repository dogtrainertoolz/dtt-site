A "hyper" dog is usually a dog whose needs aren't fully met, or who has never learned how to relax. Calmness is a skill you can teach.

## Check the basics first

- **Exercise:** is it the right kind and amount for your dog's breed and age? See [tiring out your dog](/how-to-tire-out-your-dog-easily/).
- **Mental work:** sniffing, puzzles and training often tire dogs more than running.
- **Sleep:** adult dogs need 12–14 hours a day; puppies more. Overtired dogs get wilder, not calmer.
- **Diet and health:** pain, itching and some medical issues cause restlessness. Ask your vet if something has changed.
- **Age:** adolescent dogs (about 6–18 months) are often at their wildest. See [the teenage phase](/dog-training-teenage-phase/).

## Don't overdo the excitement

Endless ball throwing or high-energy games can create a dog who's fitter, more wound-up and needs more. Balance active play with calm activities.

## Teach "settle on a mat"

1. Put down a mat or bed. Reward your dog for stepping on it, then for lying on it.
2. Reward calm: sighing, lying on their side, resting their head. Drop treats quietly between their paws.
3. Build duration while you sit nearby reading or watching TV.
4. Use the mat in different rooms, then at cafés and friends' houses. [Travel mats](amzn:portable+dog+mat+travel)

## Calming activities

- **Sniffing:** scatter feeding in the grass, snuffle mats
- **Licking:** lick mats with a little plain yogurt or wet food
- **Chewing:** appropriate long-lasting chews
- **Slow sniff walks** rather than fast-paced ones

## Reward calm moments

Catch your dog being calm (lying quietly, watching the world) and reward softly. We tend to ignore calm dogs and give attention to wild ones. Flip that around.

## Enforce naps

For puppies and adolescents, scheduled naps in a crate or pen help a lot.
